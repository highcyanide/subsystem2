CHAPTER 1 
PLANNING
1.1 Introduction
Warehouse operations depend on accurate information about what stock is available, where it is stored, which batch should be picked first, and what stage each order has reached. When these details are recorded manually or in disconnected files, staff spend time searching for information and supervisors have limited visibility into stock risks and fulfillment progress.
WIMS is a browser-based inventory and fulfillment system for a warehouse that handles transfer and export orders. It centralizes products, locations, batches, quantities, orders, users, stock adjustments, and inventory movements in a MySQL database. The system applies first-in, first-out (FIFO) allocation to eligible batches and presents role-specific views for administration, warehouse operations, and management review.
1.2 Background of the Problem
The warehouse receives products in batches and stores them in physical locations identified by zone, aisle, shelf, and bin. Products may have expiry dates, so picking the oldest eligible batch is important, especially for perishables. Orders also pass through several stages: Pending, Picking, Picked, Packed, and Shipped.
In the existing process, stock counts, batch details, order progress, and movement records may be maintained through paper forms, spreadsheets, verbal instructions, or separate files. This makes it difficult to obtain a current stock balance, locate a batch, identify low-stock items, confirm that staff picked from the correct location, or trace who performed an adjustment. The lack of a shared workflow increases the risk of wrong-item picks, expired stock usage, duplicate deductions, and delayed reporting.
1.3 Problem Statement
Warehouse administrators, staff, and owners need a reliable way to monitor inventory and fulfill orders. At present, inventory quantities, batch expiry dates, physical locations, order statuses, and stock movements are not consistently managed in one connected workflow. Staff may need to search several records before picking an item, while owners and administrators may receive delayed or incomplete information about stock levels and operational performance.
These limitations can cause inaccurate stock balances, missed low-stock conditions, inefficient picking, and avoidable picking errors. An IT solution is necessary to centralize warehouse records, enforce role-based access, prioritize eligible stock using FIFO, verify location and product scans, record every material movement, and provide timely dashboards and reports.
1.4 General Objective
To develop a web-based Warehouse Inventory Management System that improves the accuracy, traceability, and efficiency of warehouse inventory control and order fulfillment.
1.5 Specific Objectives
To authenticate users and provide role-based access for Admin, Staff, and Owner accounts.
To maintain product, warehouse location, batch, supplier, purchase order, expiry, and quantity records.
To calculate FIFO pick instructions using the oldest non-expired batches with available stock.
To provide staff with an open order queue and controlled order status workflow.
To verify the expected warehouse location and product SKU through barcode or code scanning.
To record stock adjustments and inventory movements with the responsible user, reason, quantity, and timestamp.
To identify products below their configured low-stock thresholds and display stock alerts.
To provide dashboards and summaries appropriate to each user role.
To maintain order item quantities for requested, picked, packed, and shipped work.
To reduce manual searching, duplicate recording, and errors in warehouse operations.

1.9 Limitations and Assumptions
The first release is designed for one warehouse and one MySQL database.
Users must have valid accounts and appropriate permissions.
Product, batch, location, order, and threshold data must be entered accurately.
Camera scanning depends on browser permissions, device hardware, lighting, and network access; manual code entry may be used as a fallback.
The initial authentication seed accounts are for development and demonstration. Production deployment must use changed credentials, secure configuration, HTTPS, and stronger account administration.
The system reports the data stored in it and cannot correct physical stock discrepancies without a verified adjustment.
Backup, high availability, and external integrations are deployment responsibilities beyond the initial prototype.
1.10 Feasibility Study
Technical Feasibility - The system is technically feasible because the target environment already supports PHP, Apache, MySQL, PDO, HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The application can use a layered structure consisting of configuration, authentication, controllers, services, models, and views. The required database entities and core workflow are small enough for a student or small development team to implement incrementally. Barcode or QR scanning can be provided through a browser-compatible JavaScript library, with manual entry as a fallback.
Economic Feasibility - The prototype can be developed with existing computers, XAMPP, VS Code, open-source software, and an existing local network. These choices minimize licensing costs. Future costs may include hosting, backups, HTTPS, barcode labels, scanners, training, and maintenance. The expected benefits of fewer stock errors, less manual reconciliation, and faster reporting justify the low initial development cost.
Operational Feasibility - The system matches the existing warehouse roles and activities. Staff use a focused operational view, administrators control configuration and adjustments, and owners receive summary information. Short training, clear labels, and a consistent status workflow should support adoption. A pilot using a limited set of products and one warehouse zone is recommended before full deployment.
Schedule Feasibility - The project is suitable for incremental delivery. Login and database setup can be delivered first, followed by inventory and FIFO, order fulfillment, scan verification, dashboards, testing, and documentation. A working prototype can be completed within an academic project schedule if the first release remains within the scope defined above.

2.1 Existing System Description
The current warehouse process is primarily manual or distributed across paper records, spreadsheets, and verbal coordination:
A product shipment is received and its quantity, batch, expiry date, and location are recorded.
A transfer or export request is received and staff identify the required products and quantities.
Staff search inventory records and choose a batch, usually by experience or visible availability.
Staff travel to the recorded location and pick the product.
Counts and order progress are updated manually.
A supervisor or owner reviews summaries after updates have been collected.
The process does not consistently enforce FIFO, verify the location and SKU before a pick, or connect each quantity change to a user and movement record.

2.3 Problems and Issues Identified
Inconsistent stock visibility: Records may be outdated, duplicated, or stored in different places.
FIFO and expiry risk: Staff may select a newer batch before an older eligible batch or accidentally use expired stock.
Picking errors: There is no consistent two-step check that confirms both the physical location and product SKU.
Weak traceability: Manual changes may not identify the user, reason, exact quantity, or timestamp.
Delayed alerts: Low stock and expiry risks may be noticed only during a manual review.
Slow order updates: Staff and owners may not see current progress through Pending, Picking, Picked, Packed, and Shipped stages.
Difficult reporting: Preparing summaries requires collecting and reconciling multiple records.

3.1 Proposed System Overview
WIMS is a server-rendered web application. Users access a role-specific dashboard through a browser. Controllers receive requests, services apply business rules such as FIFO and movement recording, models represent domain data, and PDO connects the application to MySQL. Views render forms, lists, alerts, dashboards, and workflow results.
The design uses one logical MySQL database with separate data responsibilities. Inventory data includes users, products, locations, batches, movements, and adjustments. Order data includes orders and order items. The logical separation supports clear DFDs without requiring separate physical databases in the prototype.

3.6 Detailed Process Design: FIFO Pick and Scan Verification
The selected major process is the fulfillment of an order item. It combines FIFO allocation, location verification, product verification, stock deduction, movement recording, and order item updates.

centercenter00

Process rules
A batch with an expiry date earlier than today is not eligible.
Eligible batches are ordered by received date, then expiry date and batch identifier as tie-breakers.
The system must confirm sufficient total eligible stock before committing the full requested allocation.
A location mismatch or SKU mismatch must stop the current verification step and leave stock unchanged.
A successful pick updates the batch quantity, records an inventory movement, and updates the order item.
Repeating a status request must not deduct the same requested quantity twice.
Insufficient stock must return a clear message so a supervisor can adjust the order or inventory.

3.7 Design Explanation
The design separates presentation, request coordination, and business rules so the system can evolve without mixing database logic into every page. The database records both current state and movement history: current quantities make daily work fast, while movements and adjustments provide traceability. FIFO is implemented as a service because it is a business rule used by order fulfillment and scan workflows.
Role-based dashboards reduce clutter and limit access to information according to responsibility. Staff receive action-oriented work queues and verification feedback. Admins receive control and configuration functions. Owners receive summary information without needing operational editing access. The responsive interface supports the devices likely to be used in a warehouse while retaining manual entry when camera scanning is unavailable.
